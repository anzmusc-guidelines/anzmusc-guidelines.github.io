# Part A. Draft EtD text

## Scope note (optional preamble for the Rationale, not an EtD domain)

**[new, modelled on rec-0 and rec-2]**

For this recommendation, the panel considered the choice of DMARD in people with RA who have had an inadequate response to, or have been unable to tolerate, conventional synthetic DMARDs (csDMARDs), including methotrexate (MTX). This population includes people who have had an inadequate response to MTX monotherapy as well as those who have had an inadequate response to combination csDMARD therapy. The panel did not consider the initial choice of DMARD in people who have not previously received DMARDs (see [rec-0: Initial DMARD therapy]) or the choice of DMARD in people who have had an inadequate response to a b/tsDMARD (see [rec-2: Choice of DMARD after TNFi]).

The panel noted that the definition of an "inadequate response" to treatment may vary between individuals, but may broadly be interpreted as persistent disease activity (i.e., falling short of the agreed treatment target) despite an adequate trial of therapy at the maximum tolerated dose, or the development of adverse effects that warrant discontinuation of the csDMARD. For MTX, an adequate trial may be considered to be approximately 3 months at a dose of at least 20 mg weekly (or the maximum tolerated dose), although this is likely to vary depending on the individual clinical circumstances, including disease severity and impact. **[reused: rec-2, adapted; CHECK that this matches the definition used in the evidence summary]**

## Values and Preferences

**Suggested rating: Substantial variability is expected or uncertain** (same as rec-0 and rec-2)

**[reused: rec-2, first paragraph, with minor adaptation]**
A systematic review of patient preferences for DMARD treatment in RA included 36 studies published between 1990 and 2018. The included studies generally involved participants who had already commenced DMARDs, and are therefore likely to be broadly applicable to people with RA who are considering a change in DMARD therapy following an inadequate response to csDMARDs (Durand 2020). The review found that, in general, people with RA consider treatment benefits (particularly improvement in symptoms and function) to be more important than both serious and non-serious adverse events (AEs). However some studies found evidence of important risk aversion (particularly regarding serious AEs), which suggests that there is considerable variation in preferences between individuals. Dosing and administration considerations, including route and frequency of medications, were generally found to be less important than treatment benefits but more important than AEs, although this also varied between and within studies. A subsequent systematic review of quantitative preference studies in RA reached similar conclusions (Simons 2022).

**[new]**
Several preference studies have specifically examined the decision facing people with RA who have not reached their treatment target with MTX. A large conjoint analysis of 1,273 people with RA in the United States, which was designed to measure preferences for triple therapy, bDMARDs and JAK inhibitors (JAKi) in people failing MTX monotherapy, identified five distinct "preference phenotypes" (Fraenkel 2018). The largest group (38%) was most strongly influenced by the cost of the medication; the next largest (26%) by the risk of bothersome side effects; a further group (11%) was most concerned about very rare but serious side effects; a small group (7%) strongly preferred oral over parenteral medications; and the remaining group (18%) was most strongly influenced by speed of onset and the risk of serious infection. The authors concluded that preferences are heterogeneous and that no single treatment option would be preferred by all patients. A multi-country discrete choice experiment of 2,090 people with RA receiving DMARD therapy in Europe and the United States similarly found that no single treatment attribute explained more than 30% of treatment preference; on average, participants were willing to accept a higher risk of serious infection and venous thromboembolism in exchange for improvements in pain, daily activities or convenience of administration, but the acceptable trade-offs varied considerably between individuals, including by age and sex (Alten 2024). In a discrete choice experiment of 228 people with RA in the United States, the chance of pain reduction was the most important attribute, followed by out-of-pocket cost, improvement in physical function and reduction in fatigue, with the risk of serious adverse events and the route and frequency of administration relatively less important; latent class analysis again identified distinct groups of patients with differing priorities (Poudel 2025).

**[reused: rec-0 and rec-2, adapted]**
A study of 152 patients with early RA that used a discrete-choice experiment design also identified important heterogeneity in preferences, with approximately half of participants being relatively risk averse (particularly to serious adverse effects) and the other half being more benefit-driven (Hazlewood 2016). It is plausible that individuals with RA who have not responded to csDMARDs may place more weight on improvement in disease activity, although there is still likely to be important variation between individuals. A systematic review of patient-based benefit-risk assessment in people with a range of chronic diseases indicates that variation in individual risk tolerance is common, and may be higher in those with greater disease severity and longer treatment experience (El Masri 2022). Patients' perceptions of the competing risks of benefits and harms are also affected by prior experience with DMARDs, including prior DMARD-related adverse effects (Chan 2021).

**[new]**
A mixed-methods study of people with inflammatory arthritis and rheumatology clinicians in the United Kingdom found that, despite awareness of the potential long-term risks associated with JAKi, patients taking these medications generally felt that the benefits outweighed the risks, and valued the convenience of an oral medication and its short half-life in the context of infection (de Souza 2024). In contrast, some clinicians in the same study expressed persisting concerns regarding the safety of JAKi.

**[new, for Sam to confirm wording]**
A living systematic review and evidence map of patient preference studies in inflammatory arthritis, designed specifically to inform guideline recommendations, is underway (Saadat 2025) and will be used to inform future updates of this living recommendation.

**[reused: rec-0 and rec-2, final paragraph]**
The panel considered that while many people would be likely to choose the treatment regimen that offers the highest chance of improving their current symptoms (including pain), there was also evidence that there is likely to be substantial variability between individuals in their preferences and values. The anticipated variability in preferences and values between patients underscores the importance of shared decision-making between clinician and patient regarding treatments for RA.

## Resources

**Suggested rating: Important issues, or potential issues not investigated** (same as rec-0 and rec-2)

**[reused: rec-0, adapted; new citations]**
We have not systematically reviewed the cost-effectiveness of the various treatment options in people with RA who have had an inadequate response to csDMARDs. There is evidence from North American settings that, in people with active RA despite MTX, a strategy of adding a bDMARD without first trying triple therapy is unlikely to be cost-effective. A cost-effectiveness analysis based on the RACAT trial (in which triple therapy was non-inferior to etanercept plus MTX) estimated that first-line etanercept plus MTX would result in 0.15 additional lifetime quality-adjusted life years at an incremental cost of US$77,290 per patient (incremental cost-effectiveness ratio of US$521,520 per QALY), and concluded that initiating biologic therapy without trying triple therapy first increases costs while providing minimal incremental benefit (Bansback 2017). A cost-effectiveness analysis based on the TEAR trial reached a similar conclusion for both immediate and step-up strategies, finding that a substantial reduction in the cost of biologic agents would be required for an etanercept-based strategy to become cost-effective (Jalal 2016). Both analyses predate the widespread availability of biosimilars and used US drug prices, and the estimates may not apply to the current Australian context. More recently, a cost-effectiveness analysis from Hong Kong found that treatment sequences commencing with a biosimilar TNF inhibitor after MTX failure were cost-effective compared with a sequence commencing with leflunomide, largely because of the lower prices of biosimilars in that setting (Peng 2024). **[CHECK: whether the evidence summary for this PICO includes any Australian or PBAC economic evaluation that should be cited here]**

**[reused: rec-0 and rec-2, merged and adapted]**
Although there are no publicly available data regarding the actual comparative costs of different b/tsDMARDs to the payer (i.e. the Federal Government) in Australia, b/tsDMARDs are generally far more expensive than csDMARDs and represent an important fraction of total pharmaceutical expenditure. Therefore the cost implications of b/tsDMARD use are likely to be an important consideration at the societal level and for funders, particularly if there are marginal differences in efficacy between b/tsDMARDs and combination csDMARD therapy. The cost difference between csDMARDs and b/tsDMARDs has narrowed with the introduction of biosimilars; the listing of TNF inhibitor biosimilars on the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) has been estimated to have saved approximately AU$560 million in public expenditure between 2015 and 2025, with most of the savings accruing in RA (Yiu 2026a), and treatment persistence with etanercept has been similar before and after the introduction of its biosimilar (Yiu 2025; Deakin 2024). While there are likely to be some cost differences between individual b/tsDMARDs, these differences may vary over time due to various factors including statutory requirements and market forces (including the advent of biosimilar drugs). The cost implications of the choice between csDMARD combinations (e.g. the addition of sulfasalazine and hydroxychloroquine, or a switch to leflunomide) are likely to be small.

**[reused: rec-0 and rec-2, adapted; CHECK PBS wording]**
At the individual patient level the direct cost implications are less profound for those who meet Government funding criteria. In Australia, PBS-subsidised treatment with a b/tsDMARD for RA requires documentation of severe active disease and of failure to achieve an adequate response to at least 6 months of intensive csDMARD therapy, which must include MTX at a dose of at least 20 mg weekly (unless contraindicated or not tolerated) in combination with at least one other csDMARD **[CHECK: exact current PBS wording, including duration of each csDMARD and the disease activity thresholds; I could not access pbs.gov.au from this session]**. People who have had an inadequate response to MTX monotherapy alone are therefore generally not eligible for subsidised b/tsDMARD therapy until they have also trialled another csDMARD, and the out-of-pocket cost of an unsubsidised b/tsDMARD is prohibitive for most. There may be some differences between medications with respect to prescription dispensing fees, particularly for combination csDMARD regimens and for bDMARDs for which co-prescription of MTX is required, although the absolute cost difference and its impact is likely to vary between individual patients.

**[reused: rec-0 and rec-2, updated with new data]**
We did not identify any data that directly compared the environmental impact of the treatment options considered in this recommendation. A recent life cycle assessment estimated that monoclonal antibody-based medicines have a mean carbon footprint of approximately 370 kg CO2-equivalent per month of treatment, compared with approximately 14 kg CO2-equivalent per month for an oral treatment, and that the carbon footprint of second-line treatment options in RA ranges from about 290 to 2,050 kg CO2-equivalent per year depending on the agent (Taillemite 2026). It is therefore plausible that combination therapy with bDMARDs has a larger negative impact on the environment than csDMARDs or oral tsDMARDs due to manufacturing, packaging and transport, however the magnitude of any difference in the Australian context, and other indirect effects, are currently unknown.

## Equity

**Suggested rating: Important issues, or potential issues not investigated** (same as rec-0 and rec-2)

**[reused: rec-0 and rec-2, verbatim with population adapted]**
There are a number of factors that may influence health opportunities and outcomes in individual patients. Such factors (based on the PROGRESS-Plus characteristics) might include poor health literacy; residence in rural, remote or relatively under-serviced locations; primary language other than English; low educational attainment; the presence of disability; or adverse socioeconomic or social circumstances. Some of these may be important considerations in the choice of subsequent DMARD therapy, particularly DMARD regimens that involve complex dosing, regular dose adjustments, frequent monitoring for adverse effects, or close specialist supervision, and as a result may have an impact on equity if the opportunity to access the various treatment options varies between groups.

**[new]**
An Australian inception cohort study of people with early RA managed within a protocolised treat-to-target framework found that residence in an area of greater socioeconomic disadvantage was associated with higher disease activity, greater disability and poorer quality of life over time, despite similar rates of b/tsDMARD initiation and switching across socioeconomic quintiles (Russell 2026). This suggests that inequity in outcomes may persist even where access to subsidised medicines is equivalent, and that the choice of DMARD regimen is only one of several factors that influence outcomes in disadvantaged groups.

**[reused: rec-0, adapted; new data]**
The panel noted the importance of access to a rheumatologist. In Australia, PBS-subsidised b/tsDMARDs for RA may only be prescribed by a rheumatologist or a clinical immunologist with expertise in the management of RA **[CHECK]**, and the application process requires documentation of prior csDMARD therapy and disease activity. The Australian rheumatology workforce is substantially smaller than that of comparable countries and is unevenly distributed, with regional and rural areas most affected; the Australian Rheumatology Association has estimated a current shortfall of approximately 240 adult rheumatologists **[CHECK figure against the ARA 2025 workforce report]**. People who are less able to access timely specialist care may therefore experience delays in escalation of DMARD therapy, including commencement of a b/tsDMARD, and may be more likely to be managed with csDMARDs alone or with glucocorticoids in the interim. There are few data regarding RA and its treatment in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people, and access to rheumatology care is a recognised area of need in this population (Sines 2024).

**[reused: rec-0, verbatim]**
The panel also noted that some members of the Australian community do not have access to Medicare (e.g., refugees, international students). These people may experience additional barriers to the use of b/tsDMARDs and of combination DMARD therapy (including the cost of medical appointments and medications), which may lead to additional health inequity.

## Acceptability

**Suggested rating: Important issues, or potential issues not investigated** (as for rec-0; rec-2 used "No important issues with the recommended alternative". See Part D for the reasoning behind this suggestion.)

**[reused: rec-2, adapted]**
It is likely that a change in DMARD therapy would be acceptable to most people with active RA who have not responded to csDMARDs, although this may not apply equally to all treatment options and there is likely to be variation between individuals based on their particular circumstances, prior treatment experience, and preferences. In general, people with RA are more likely to accept changes to their treatment regimen when they perceive that the change may result in an important improvement in their current symptoms and future prognosis (Chan 2021). There is some evidence that people with RA often prefer medications administered orally, although this preference varies according to age, duration of disease, and other factors (Taylor 2021; Alten 2024).

**[reused: rec-0, adapted]**
There is evidence of considerable variation between rheumatologists in the use of combination csDMARD therapy, including triple therapy, in RA (Pokharel 2021; Harris 2013; Edwards 2012). Rheumatologists' beliefs regarding the effectiveness of triple therapy have been shown to vary considerably and to impact on their interpretation of the evidence base and choice of treatment options (Pokharel 2021). A study that compared RA treatment recommendations developed by separate panels comprised of either consumers or clinicians found that when presented with the same data regarding the benefits and harms of triple therapy versus methotrexate monotherapy, the consumer panel conditionally favoured triple therapy whereas the clinician panel conditionally favoured MTX monotherapy (Fraenkel 2016), although this study considered DMARD-naive rather than MTX-experienced patients.

**[new]**
A systematic review and thematic synthesis of qualitative studies of clinicians' DMARD prescribing found that rheumatologists' prescribing is influenced by patients' characteristics and preferences, by the rheumatologist's own knowledge, experience, habits and subjective judgements, by the demands on consultation time, and by the costs and complexity of medication funding arrangements (Lalor 2022). The acceptability of JAKi to clinicians has been influenced by regulatory safety warnings following the ORAL Surveillance trial. In Australia, the Therapeutic Goods Administration applied a class-wide boxed warning to all JAKi in 2023, advising that they should be used in people aged 65 years or older, those at increased risk of major cardiovascular events or cancer, and current or past long-term smokers only if no suitable treatment alternatives are available (TGA 2023). The 2025 update of the EULAR recommendations for the management of RA similarly recommends that, after an inadequate response to csDMARDs, a bDMARD should be added, with JAKi considered only after careful consideration of risks including major cardiovascular events, malignancy and thromboembolic events (Smolen 2026). Data from international RA registers show that initiations of JAKi slowed and discontinuations increased following the regulatory warnings, particularly for tofacitinib (Mongin 2026), and an audit from a metropolitan Australian service found a similar decline in new JAKi prescriptions following the United States boxed warning (Kwan 2026). Nevertheless, JAKi remain widely used as the first b/tsDMARD in Australian practice (Ciciriello 2024; Youssef 2025; Scheepers 2024), and the UK mixed-methods study described above found that patients established on JAKi were generally satisfied with their treatment (de Souza 2024).

**[reused: rec-0, verbatim, for the panel to confirm or update]**
The consumer panellist noted that many people who have been recently diagnosed with RA are anxious about commencing MTX and that, in comparison, the additional burden of combination therapies in addition to MTX is often considered to be relatively minor. *[Sam: this came from the DMARD-naive discussion; the consumer panellists may wish to comment on the acceptability of adding further csDMARDs versus commencing a b/tsDMARD in people who have already been taking MTX for some months.]*

## Feasibility

**Suggested rating: Important issues, or potential issues not investigated** (as for rec-0; see Part D)

**[reused: rec-0 and rec-2, merged and adapted; CHECK PBS wording]**
In people with RA who have had an inadequate response to csDMARDs, both the addition of further csDMARDs (including triple therapy) and the commencement of a b/tsDMARD are consistent with current rheumatology practice in Australia. However, PBS subsidy for b/tsDMARDs in RA is restricted to people with severe active disease who have failed to achieve an adequate response to at least 6 months of intensive csDMARD therapy including MTX (at a dose of at least 20 mg weekly, unless contraindicated or not tolerated) in combination with at least one other csDMARD **[CHECK exact wording]**, and only one b/tsDMARD may be subsidised at any one time. # Part B. New literature since the source recommendations (annotated)

Search approach: PubMed (via the PubMed connector) and targeted web searches, 20 November 2026, limited to 2022 onwards (the DMARD-naive panel met in December 2021; the TNFi-IR panel met in November 2022), plus a small number of older papers that are directly on-topic for the csDMARD-IR decision and were not cited in either source recommendation. This was a pragmatic search, not a systematic one. Items are grouped by the EtD domain they best inform.

## Values and preferences

- **Fraenkel 2018, Ann Rheum Dis (older, but directly on-topic and not previously cited).** Conjoint analysis, 1,273 US patients with RA, designed around the decision for patients failing MTX monotherapy: triple therapy vs bDMARD vs JAKi. Attributes: administration, onset, bothersome side effects, serious infection, very rare side effects, amount of information, cost. Five preference phenotypes (cost-driven 38%; bothersome-side-effect averse 26%; rare-serious-side-effect averse 11%; strong oral preference 7%; onset and serious infection 18%). This is the single most relevant preference study for the new PICO.
- **Alten 2024, RMD Open.** Online DCE, 2,090 patients in Europe and the US on systemic DMARDs (male patients oversampled). No single attribute explained more than 30% of preference. Patients willing to accept higher risk of serious infection and blood clots for improvement in pain, daily activities or administration convenience, but trade-offs varied; women most concerned about blood clots and serious infections; patients aged 18 to 44 placed less weight on frequency and mode of administration. Industry-funded (Galapagos).
- **Poudel 2025, Patient Prefer Adherence.** US web DCE, 228 patients. Relative importance: pain reduction 2.4, out-of-pocket cost 2.1, physical function 1.6, fatigue 1.5, serious AEs 0.6, route/frequency 0.09. Two latent classes (fatigue-driven vs cost-driven).
- **Simons 2022, Arthritis Res Ther.** Systematic review of 23 quantitative preference studies (20 of RA treatment). Benefits, risks, administration method and cost were all important; benefit was more important than risk in about half of studies. Confirms Durand 2020 conclusions with a slightly later search.
- **Saadat 2025, BMJ Open.** Protocol for a living systematic review and evidence map of patient preference studies in inflammatory arthritis to inform guidelines (you and Rachelle are co-authors). Worth a sentence so the recommendation points to it for future updates.
- **de Souza 2024, BMC Rheumatol.** UK mixed-methods study (surveys and interviews, Sept 2021 to Jan 2022). 39% of patients on JAKi "very satisfied" vs 25% on biologics; patients valued oral route and short half-life; felt benefits outweighed risks; clinicians had lingering safety concerns. Also informs Acceptability.
- **Raskin 2025, Semin Arthritis Rheum** (10.1016/j.semarthrit.2025.152769). Rapid review of 11 decision aids and 27 preference studies for OMERACT health outcome descriptors; shows how variably outcomes are described to patients. Background only; not cited in the draft.
- **Curtis 2026, J Patient Exp** (10.1177/23743735251415236). US DCE of 354 patients comparing drugs with an implantable neuroimmune device; includes a biologic-naive stratum. Key drivers: out-of-pocket cost, symptom improvement, function, fatigue, joint damage protection. Peripheral; not cited.
- **Chan 2025, Arthritis Care Res** (10.1002/acr.25437). NZ DCE on bDMARD tapering. Not relevant to this PICO (relevant to the tapering recs).

## C1. rec-0: Initial DMARD therapy for people with rheumatoid arthritis (panel meeting 15 December 2021)

**Recommendation:** Consider using methotrexate in combination with other DMARDs as initial therapy in people with rheumatoid arthritis. For most people this implies triple therapy (methotrexate + sulfasalazine + hydroxychloroquine). While the combination of methotrexate and most b/tsDMARDs is also effective, this is currently not a feasible option for most people.

### Values and Preferences (Substantial variability is expected or uncertain)

A recent systematic review of patient preferences for DMARD treatment in RA included 36 studies published between 1990 and 2018, although the included studies generally involved those who had already commenced DMARDs and only two studies involved participants with early RA (i.e., those who are most likely to be making decisions about the initial choice of DMARD) [35 Durand 2020, 10.3899/jrheum.181165]. The review found that, in general, people with RA consider treatment benefits (particularly improvement in symptoms and function) to be more important than both serious and non-serious adverse events (AEs). However some studies found evidence of important risk aversion (particularly regarding serious AEs), which suggests that there is considerable variation in preferences between individuals. Dosing and administration considerations, including route and frequency of medications, were generally found to be less important than treatment benefits but more important than AEs, although this also varied between and within studies. Overall, the results suggest that individual preferences are likely to be an important factor in shared decision-making regarding treatments for RA.

A study of 152 patients with early RA (<2 years since diagnosis) that used a discrete-choice experiment design also identified important heterogeneity in preferences [123 Hazlewood 2016, 10.1093/rheumatology/kew280]. In general, treatment benefits were considered the most important treatment attribute, however latent-class analysis also identified that approximately half of the patients were more risk averse (particularly to serious adverse effects) in comparison to the other half who were more benefit-driven. In this study sample, participants would be willing to tolerate an increase in pill burden (triple therapy versus weekly oral medication) for a relatively small increment in treatment benefits (<5% absolute increase in chance of major symptom improvement), although the incremental improvement required was considerably higher for those in the risk averse group.

The panel considered that while many people would be likely to choose the treatment regimen that offers the highest chance of improving their current symptoms (including pain), there was also evidence that there is likely to be substantial variability between individuals in their preferences and values.

### Resources (Important issues, or potential issues not investigated)

We have not systematically reviewed the cost-effectiveness of methotrexate monotherapy versus combination therapy in DMARD-naïve patients with rheumatoid arthritis. There is some evidence that the use of triple therapy as the initial therapy is more cost-effective than initial therapy with methotrexate monotherapy in people with very early inflammatory arthritis in a European setting [122 de Jong 2016, 10.1093/rheumatology/kew321], although the estimates in this population (in which relatively large doses of glucocorticoids were used as an adjunct to csDMARDs) may not apply to the current Australian context.

Although there are no publicly available data regarding the actual comparative costs of different bDMARDs to the payer (i.e. the Federal Government) in Australia, b/tsDMARDs are generally far more expensive than csDMARDs and therefore the cost implications of b/tsDMARD use are likely to be an important consideration at the societal level and for funders, particularly if there are marginal differences in efficacy.

At the individual patient level the direct cost implications are less profound for those who meet Government funding criteria, however currently DMARD-naïve patients with RA are not eligible for subsidised treatment with any b/tsDMARD in Australia. The impact of prescription dispensing fees may be higher for those using combination csDMARDs versus monotherapy, although the absolute cost difference and its impact is likely to vary between individual patients.

We did not identify any data regarding the impact of methotrexate monotherapy versus combination therapy with csDMARDs or b/tsDMARDs on the environment. It is plausible that combination therapy (particularly with bDMARDs) may have a larger negative impact on the environment due to manufacturing, packaging and transport, however the magnitude of such an impact (if present) or other indirect effects is currently unknown.

The panel discussed the implications of the large cost difference between csDMARDs and b/tsDMARDs. The cost implications of MTX monotherapy versus combination therapy with csDMARDs are likely to be small, but the current cost of b/tsDMARDs would have a major impact on their potential use as an initial therapy for RA.

### Equity (Important issues, or potential issues not investigated)

There are a number of factors that may influence the health opportunities and outcomes in individual patients. Such factors (based on the PROGRESS-Plus characteristics) might include poor health literacy; residence in rural, remote or relatively under-serviced locations; primary language other than English; low educational attainment; the presence of disability; or adverse socioeconomic or social circumstances. Some of these may be important considerations in the choice of initial DMARD therapy, particularly DMARD regimens that involve complex dosing, regular dose adjustments, or frequent monitoring for adverse effects, and as a result may have an impact on equity if the opportunity to access initial combination therapy varies between groups.

The panel noted the importance of access to a rheumatologist. It considered that while some other practitioners (e.g., GPs, general physicians) may consider prescription of csDMARD monotherapy for people with early RA who are unable to promptly access a rheumatologist, initial combination therapy is likely to require more specialist expertise. As such, people who are less able to access immediate specialist care may be less likely to receive combination therapy.

The panel also noted that some members of the Australian community do not have access to Medicare (e.g., refugees, international students). These people may experience additional barriers to the use of combination DMARD therapy (including the cost of medical appointments and medications), which may lead to additional health inequity.

### Acceptability (Important issues, or potential issues not investigated)

There is evidence to suggest variation in the use of csDMARDs in combination versus monotherapy as initial therapy for RA [125 Pokharel 2021, 10.1093/rheumatology/keaa803] [129 Harris 2013, 10.3899/jrheum.121316] [130 Edwards 2012, 10.1136/bmjopen-2012-001603]. It is likely that this heterogeneity in practice is influenced by several factors, including variation in the preferences of patients and prescribers for the various treatment options. Rheumatologists' beliefs regarding the effectiveness of triple therapy have been shown to vary considerably and to impact on their interpretation of the evidence base and choice of treatment options [125 Pokharel 2021].

A study that compared RA treatment recommendations developed by separate panels comprised of either consumers or clinicians found that when presented with the same data regarding the benefits and harms of triple therapy versus methotrexate monotherapy in DMARD-naïve RA, the consumer panel conditionally favoured triple therapy whereas the clinician panel conditionally favoured MTX monotherapy [121 Fraenkel 2016, 10.1002/acr.22758].

A discrete choice experiment that asked patients with early RA to choose between hypothetical treatment options estimated that, after weighing network meta-analysis data on benefits and harms in addition to other considerations including dosing, the majority of patients (78%) would prefer triple therapy over MTX monotherapy as the initial treatment for RA [128 Hazlewood 2017, 10.1016/j.jclinepi.2017.10.003].

The consumer panellist noted that many people who have been recently diagnosed with RA are anxious about commencing MTX and that, in comparison, the additional burden of combination therapies in addition to MTX is often considered to be relatively minor.

### Feasibility (Important issues, or potential issues not investigated)

Initial treatment of rheumatoid arthritis with methotrexate alone or in combination with other csDMARDs is consistent with current practice in Australia and there are unlikely to be important feasibility implications. Use of b/tsDMARDs as part of an initial treatment combination in DMARD-naïve patients does not meet current subsidised funding criteria in Australia and therefore would not be feasible to widely implement. As a result of this dichotomy, the panel voted in favour of 'important issues or potential issues not investigated'.

## C2. rec-2: Choice of DMARD in people with rheumatoid arthritis who have not responded to TNFi (panel meeting 3 November 2022)

**Recommendation:** In people with RA who have had an inadequate response to one TNFi, consider switching therapy to an alternative b/tsDMARD. Consider either a different TNFi or a b/tsDMARD with a different mechanism of action. The choice of b/tsDMARD should be a shared decision between clinician and patient based on individual values, preferences, clinical status, and an assessment of risk. In particular, JAK inhibitors may not be preferred in those at a higher risk of cardiovascular disease or cancer.

### Values and Preferences (Substantial variability is expected or uncertain)

A systematic review of patient preferences for DMARD treatment in RA was identified that included 36 studies published between 1990 and 2018. The included studies generally involved participants who had already commenced DMARDs, including bDMARDs [84 Durand 2020, 10.3899/jrheum.181165]. The review found that, in general, people with RA consider treatment benefits (particularly improvement in symptoms and function) to be more important than both serious and non-serious adverse events. However some studies found evidence of important risk aversion (particularly regarding serious adverse events), which suggests that there is considerable variation in preferences between individuals. Dosing and administration considerations, including route and frequency of medications, were generally found to be less important than treatment benefits but more important than AEs, although this also varied between and within studies.

It is plausible that individuals with RA who have not responded to a TNF inhibitor may place more weight on improvement in disease activity, although there is still likely to be important variation between individuals. A recent systematic review of patient-based benefit-risk assessment in people with a range of chronic diseases indicates that variation in individual risk tolerance is common, and may be higher in those with greater disease severity and longer treatment experience [181 El Masri 2022, 10.2147/PPA.S375062]. Patients' perception of competing risks of benefits and harms are also affected by prior experience with DMARDs, including prior DMARD-related adverse effects [183 Chan 2021, 10.1007/s40271-020-00488-7].

The anticipated variability in preferences and values between patients underscores the importance of shared decision-making between clinician and patient regarding treatments for RA.

### Resources (Important issues, or potential issues not investigated)

We have not systematically reviewed the relative cost-effectiveness of the various interventions included in the current network meta-analysis. There are few existing data on the comparative cost-effectiveness of different sequences of b/tsDMARDs in RA [180 Ghabri 2020, 10.1007/s40273-020-00887-6]. However, in patients for whom a decision to institute b/tsDMARD therapy has already been taken, resource implications are unlikely to be a major determinant of the choice of subsequent DMARDs unless there is a clear difference in cost-effectiveness between the available alternative interventions.

Although there are no publicly available data regarding the actual comparative costs of different bDMARDs to the payer (i.e. the Federal Government) in Australia, b/tsDMARDs are expensive therapies and represent an important fraction of total pharmaceutical expenditure. While there are likely to be some cost differences between b/tsDMARDs, these differences may vary over time due to various factors including statutory requirements and market forces (including the advent of biosimilar drugs), and represent a relatively small proportion of the total cost. Therefore the cost implications of the choice between individual b/tsDMARDs are currently unlikely to be an important consideration at the societal level and for funders, particularly if there are marginal differences in efficacy, although this may vary over time.

At the individual patient level the direct cost implications are less profound for those who meet Government funding criteria. Currently, almost all Australian patients with RA who have had an inadequate response to a TNFi are eligible for subsidised treatment with an alternative b/tsDMARD. There may be some differences between medications with respect to prescription dispensing fees, particularly for bDMARDs for which co-prescription of methotrexate is required, although the absolute cost difference and its impact is likely to vary between individual patients.

We did not identify any data regarding the differential impact of the included interventions on the environment. It is plausible that some b/tsDMARDs (e.g. those that are administered via single-use plastic devices) may have a larger negative impact on the environment due to manufacturing, packaging and transport, however the magnitude of such an impact (if present) or other indirect effects is currently unknown.

### Equity (Important issues, or potential issues not investigated)

There are a number of factors that may influence health opportunities and outcomes in individual patients. Such factors (based on the PROGRESS-Plus characteristics) might include poor health literacy; residence in rural, remote or relatively under-serviced locations; primary language other than English; low educational attainment; the presence of disability; or adverse socioeconomic or social circumstances. Some of these may be important factors in the choice of subsequent DMARD therapy, particularly DMARD regimens that involve complex dosing, regular dose adjustments, or close specialist supervision, and may have a particular impact on equity if the opportunity to access specialist care varies between groups.

### Acceptability (No important issues with the recommended alternative)

It is likely that a switch to an alternative DMARD would be acceptable to most people with active RA who have not responded to a TNF inhibitor, although this may not apply equally to all treatment options and there may be variation between individuals based on their particular circumstances, prior treatment experience, and preferences. There is some evidence that people with RA often prefer medications administered orally, although this preference varies according to age, duration of disease, and other factors [182 Taylor 2021, 10.2147/PPA.S289692]. In general, people with RA are more likely to accept changes to their treatment regimen when they perceive that the change may result in an important improvement in their current symptoms and future prognosis [183 Chan 2021, 10.1007/s40271-020-00488-7].

### Feasibility (No important issues with the recommended alternative)

In people with RA, switching from an ineffective or poorly-tolerated TNFi to an alternative b/tsDMARD (including a switch to a different TNFi) is consistent with current rheumatology practice in Australia. All of the interventions included in the network meta-analysis that informs this recommendation (aside from sarilumab) are currently subsidised under the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme for the treatment of active RA in people who have had an inadequate response to a TNF inhibitor. For this reason, there are unlikely to be important feasibility implications.
